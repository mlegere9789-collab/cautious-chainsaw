# C-008: Lee's attack order

Researched 2026-11-24. Result: about 11:00 on 2 July 1863, Lee's order to Longstreet to attack the enemy's left, not later than about noon. The hour is Longstreet's memory alone, so the value is inferred, not documented. The evidence is weighed, not settled.

## The dispute

The plan gives two hours for Lee's order to attack on the second day: about 09:00 (from a forum thread the plan itself flags weak) and 11:00, and takes 11:00 as its default. The first pass kept 11:00 as a single memoir witness bounded by the Official Records. The questions here: who gives the hour, how well does he know it, does any report of the time give one, and what does 09:00 rest on.

## What each writer says

1. **Longstreet**, commanding the First Corps, the man the order was given to. In his article of 1877, printed in The Annals of the War (1879, p. 422): Lee came back from Ewell's lines "at about nine o'clock" and "It was fully eleven o'clock when General Lee arrived at this conclusion and ordered the movement"; the corps then waited about forty minutes for Law's brigade. In his book of 1896 (p. 365): "It was eleven o'clock when General Lee's order was issued, but he had ordered Law's brigade to its division"; and (p. 382) Lee said nothing of the march "until he gave his orders at eleven o'clock for the move to his right". He wrote 14 and 33 years later, on the Confederate clock, to answer the charge that he was ordered to attack at sunrise. "Fully" leaves room for a little after eleven.
2. **Longstreet's report of 27 July 1863** (Official Records 27/2, p. 358): "Law's brigade was ordered forward to its division during the day, and joined about noon on the 2d. Previous to his joining, I received instructions from the commanding general to move". No hour, but a ceiling: before about noon.
3. **Kershaw**, report of 1 October 1863 (p. 366): his brigade was "halted until about noon. We were then directed to move under cover of the hills toward the right". That is the march order reaching a brigade, after Lee's order to Longstreet and the wait for Law.
4. **Pendleton**, chief of artillery, report of 12 September 1863 (p. 350): after sunrise he understood "the purpose of the commanding general" to be an attack on the enemy's left, and "about midday General Longstreet arrived and viewed the ground" on the right. No hour for the order.
5. **Alexander**, of the First Corps artillery, in his book of 1907 (p. 391): Lee rode left "about nine o'clock", returned about ten, and "About 11 A.M., his orders were issued". He reached the field at 9 a.m. and was not at Lee's headquarters; he does not say how he knew the hour, and his sequence is Longstreet's. His report of 3 August 1863 (p. 429) says only that "Shortly after our arrival" (at 9 a.m.) Longstreet directed him to accompany the divisions "in the attack upon the left".
6. **Fremantle**, British observer, diary of 2 July: "At 7 A.M. I rode over part of the ground with General Longstreet, and saw him disposing M'Laws's division for to-day's fight", and "the whole morning was evidently to be occupied in disposing the troops for the attack". **Ewell**, report: a communication from Lee that "he intended the main attack to be made by the First Corps", early in the morning, no hour. **McLaws**, address of 1879: soon after his division arrived early in the morning Lee himself told him "I wish you to place your division across this road". So the intent was known in the morning.
7. **Freeman** (1935, p. 92): "there are abundant witnesses that Lee was at Ewell's headquarters about 9 A.M."; he calls Longstreet's nine for the return an impossibility. **Hood**, letter of 1875 from memory (p. 57): the decision to go on without Pickett "about 3 o'clock" in the afternoon.
8. **Taylor, Marshall, Long and Venable**, letters of 1875: none had heard of a sunrise order; none gives an hour for the order of the day.

Not read: the Civil War Talk thread and the Epperson timeline that the plan cites (neither host is reachable).

## Weighing

Only one line of knowledge gives an hour: Longstreet, and Alexander and Hoke (1887) repeat him. The reports of 1863 outrank memoirs, but none states an hour; together they put the order before about noon, and eleven fits. The reports of Alexander and Ewell are better classed than the memoirs, but they date Longstreet's order to Alexander and a message to Ewell in the morning, not Lee's formal order to Longstreet; the plan to attack the left was known by then, which is why the early plan is listed as its own value. The reported 20 minutes between the two armies' clocks is not applied and cannot turn eleven into nine.

The plan's 09:00 has no source found. The nearest nine o'clock in the record is Lee's trip to Ewell's lines (Longstreet: back at nine; Alexander: out at nine; Freeman: still there), the artillery's arrival, and the engineer Johnston joining the head of the corps, which Fitz Lee printed in 1878 on his authority.

## Decision

Class **weighted**, tier **inferred**. Default: **about 11:00**, before about noon, with the flags single source and memoir only. The plan's default holds, and so does the first delegated default. Other values stay selectable with their reasons: about 09:00 (forum, no source found), not later than about noon (the ceiling), early morning (the plan already known), about 09:00 (Lee's return, not the order), about 15:00 (Hood, contradicted by the reports). The order drives no unit, arrival or gate.

## What would change it

A report, diary, log or dispatch of 2 July that gives the hour of the order, Johnston's own account, or a statement by someone with Lee that morning (Taylor, Marshall, Venable, Long), or the forum and timeline pages if they name a primary source.

## Searches that failed

No hour for Lee's order in the Official Records reports named above, nor in any local text outside Longstreet and those who repeat him. The books of Long, Taylor and Sorrel were searched, not read through. Johnston's own letter was not found. Newspapers of 1863 were not searched.
