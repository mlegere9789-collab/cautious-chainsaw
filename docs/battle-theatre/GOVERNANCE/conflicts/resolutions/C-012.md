# C-012. Vincent's lead over Law at Little Round Top

Researched 24 November 2026. Default: 10 minutes. Class: weighted (the evidence favours one value but does not settle it). Tier: inferred (reasoned from one recollection; no writing of the time states it).

## The dispute

On the afternoon of 2 July 1863 Colonel Strong Vincent's brigade (16th Michigan, 44th New York, 83rd Pennsylvania, 20th Maine) hurried to the southern slope of Little Round Top, and the Confederates of Hood's division, Law's Alabamians and Robertson's Texans, came up against it. The plan says the brigade took its place "about 10 minutes" before the Confederates arrived (one web encyclopaedia) or "15 minutes" (one magazine page), and recommends 10. The first pass found no source for either. This pass looked for who first printed a figure and for what the officers on the hill wrote.

## What the witnesses say

**Colonel William C. Oates, 15th Alabama (Law's brigade): 10 minutes.** Writing in 1905, 42 years after, he says Vincent's brigade "reached this position ten minutes before my arrival" (his book, p. 214; Norton prints the same page in 1913, p. 89). The position is the ledge of rocks at the foot of Little Round Top where his regiment met the 20th Maine. Oates led the column that came round Big Round Top, so the figure is the lead over that column, not over the first attackers. He stood on the Confederate side and cannot have seen the brigade arrive; he does not say how he knew. He gives no clock for it (his book has the guns opening about 3:30 p.m.).

**The officers of the brigade, July and August 1863: no figure.** Colonel Rice, writing on 31 July, says the brigade was "ordered into position at about 4 p. m." and "had scarcely formed line of battle and pushed forward its skirmishers when" Hood's division attacked (pp. 616-617). Colonel Chamberlain, 6 July, says his "formation was scarcely complete" when an infantry assault fell on the centre of the brigade, and that only later did he see a body of the enemy moving round from the foot of Great Round Top toward his left (p. 623). Lieutenant Colonel Welch (16th Michigan) and Major Lamont (83rd Pennsylvania) also say about 4 p.m. All were on the hill and knew from their own commands. This is the best class of writing there is, but none of it states an interval; it shows that the first attack came very soon after the line formed and the flanking column later.

**Captain Woodward, 83rd Pennsylvania, 6 July 1863: 45 minutes, by a clock he later disowned.** His report has the regiment ordered into position about 2:30 p.m. and the enemy engaging his skirmishers at 3:15 (p. 632). Norton, a private of the same brigade, says the times are wrong and that Woodward admitted it in a letter of 12 January 1912 (p. 246). Woodward's own regiment's second report (Lamont, 14 August) and the reports of Rice and Welch say about 4 p.m.

**An officer of the brigade staff, 1900: "a few minutes".** The chapter on the 44th New York in New York at Gettysburg says the brigade, "by marching in double time, reached Little Round Top a few minutes before the Confederates" (vol. 1, p. 370). The volume does not print the writer's name; the writer says they were the brigade's assistant inspector general. No figure, and no reason given for the judgment.

**H. S. Melcher, 20th Maine, 1885 (printed in Battles and Leaders): a ten-minute halt.** He writes that the two Alabama regiments halted "some ten minutes on the crest" of Round Top and that this halt let Vincent's brigade reach the hill in time (p. 314). That is the length of the Confederates' halt, not a count of Vincent's lead. Oates himself had written in 1878 that his halt on Round Top lasted "less than ten minutes" (Southern Historical Society Papers, vol. 6, p. 175) and said nothing about Vincent's arrival. So "ten minutes" was already in print for another interval before Oates used it for the lead in 1905; whether his sentence rests on a separate memory cannot be told from the record.

**Fifteen minutes.** No report, diary or book read gives it for this interval. The fifteen-minute figures near the subject time the artillery fire before the advance (Law, in Battles and Leaders; Norton). The plan took it from a magazine page by Phifer, which could not be read here, and a web page is only a pointer.

## The weighing

- The writers of the time give no figure. The only figure comes from a recollection 42 years later by a man who was at the ledge but could not have seen the arrival and does not say how he knew. That makes ten minutes the best figure there is, and not a strong one.
- Fifteen has no witness. The 45 minutes rest on one report that its writer withdrew and that three reports of the same brigade contradict. "A few minutes" agrees with ten and measures nothing.
- One line of knowledge carries ten minutes (Oates's book and Norton's reprint of it), so the value is marked single-source and memoir-only. It is not documented.
- The clocks cannot decide: the Union reports say about 4 p.m. for the order, Norton reads the attack at 4:30 to 5, Major Bane of the 4th Texas has the order to advance at about 4:30, and each reading carries an "about" as large as the interval itself. The reported difference of about 20 minutes between the two armies' clocks is not applied.
- A lead is not the same for every regiment: the Union reports put the first attack on the centre of the brigade sooner after the line formed than the flanking column on its left.

## The decision

The default is 10 minutes: Vincent's brigade is drawn on the ledge ten minutes before the 15th and 47th Alabama come up to it, and the first attacks on the centre of the brigade come sooner. The range shown is 10 to 15, the two figures of the plan's row; ten has a witness, fifteen has none. It is chosen because it is the only figure any witness gives, not because the plan recommends it. The other three values stay listed with their reasons: 15 minutes (no source), about 45 minutes (disowned clock) and "a few minutes" (no figure).

The plan's recommended default (10 minutes) holds as a figure, but as inferred, not documented, and as the lead over the flanking column. The first delegated default kept 10 minutes as a marked stand-in with no source: the figure holds, the marking does not, because Oates's book states it.

## What would change it

A report, diary or letter of 1863 to 1864 that gives clock readings or minutes for both the arrival of Vincent's brigade and the first Confederate contact would replace the recollection of 1905. So would earlier letters of Oates, Chamberlain or Law (Oates's correspondence to the battlefield commission, the Bachelder papers) if one gives the lead, or the sources behind the figures in the plan's two web pages. A figure of fifteen minutes with a source would move the default.

## Searches that failed

No interval for Vincent's lead was found in the Official Records (reports of the Third Brigade, of Barnes and Sykes, and of Law's and Robertson's brigades), in Law's essay in Battles and Leaders, in Powell's history of the Fifth Corps (1896), in the Joint Committee's volume of 1865, in Chamberlain's book of 1915, in the Park Service texts on the Round Tops, or anywhere in the local library by a search for ten, fifteen, twenty or a few minutes near Vincent and Round Top. The Wikipedia and Warfare History Network pages the plan names were not read (hosts not reachable here), the Gettysburg park archive and the Bachelder papers were not read, and one attempt to view the page image of Battles and Leaders, p. 314, failed.
