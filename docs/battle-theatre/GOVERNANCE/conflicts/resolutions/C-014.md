# C-014. Prisoners taken, 20th Maine front

Researched 24 November 2026. Default: 368 to 400 prisoners sent to the rear after the 20th Maine's counter-charge on 2 July 1863, with the 25 taken in the night advance kept apart. Class: irreducible (two reports of the time, by Chamberlain and Barnes, give 400 and 368 and nothing separates them; Confederate evidence points lower). Tier: documented (two reports of 1863 state the figures).

## The dispute

In the late afternoon of 2 July (the hour is conflict C-013), out of ammunition and outflanked on the left of Little Round Top, Colonel Chamberlain ordered the 20th Maine to fix bayonets and charge. The Alabamians and Texans in front of it broke, and many surrendered. The plan gives two answers for how many: a range of 218 to 368 (recommended) and "400+25". The first pass found 368 (Barnes) and 400 plus 25 (Chamberlain), and no source for 218.

## What the witnesses say

**Colonel Chamberlain, report of 6 July 1863 (four days after): four hundred.** After the charge and the return to the position, "Four hundred prisoners, including two field and several line officers, were sent to the rear", mainly from the 15th and 47th Alabama with some of the 4th and 5th Texas (Official Records, p. 625). Later, in the night advance up Great Round Top (after dark), "we laid hold of 25 prisoners". He gives no method for the 400 and does not say whether wounded men are in it.

**General Barnes, division commander, report of 24 August 1863 (53 days after): 368.** After the charge, "368 prisoners, including 1 colonel, 1 lieutenant-colonel, and a dozen other officers of lesser rank were sent to the rear" (p. 603). He was not at the ledge and says the story of Vincent's brigade is "drawn principally from the report of its commander"; he does not say how the 368 was counted.

**Colonel Rice, brigade commander, report of 31 July 1863: over 500 for the whole brigade.** By 9 p.m. the brigade "had taken and sent to the rear over 500 prisoners, including 2 colonels and 15 commissioned officers" (p. 618). That is four regiments and the evening advance. Colonel Fisher (9 July) says his brigade's two regiments, with the 20th Maine, took "over 30 prisoners in our ascent" of Great Round Top at 10 p.m. (p. 658); Chamberlain's 25 very probably lies inside that 30 and the two should not be added.

**The regiment's own survivors: about 300.** Their monument of 1886, printed in the Maine volume (1898), says the regiment charged "capturing 302 prisoners" (p. 249). Lieutenant Melcher (Battles and Leaders, 1888) has the Alabama colonel fall into their hands "with over three hundred prisoners and all the wounded"; Private Gerrish (1882) has "over three hundred"; a guidebook of 1892 says "over 300". None names a source.

**Colonel Oates, 15th Alabama, who fought them: far fewer.** In his book of 1905 (p. 216) he answers Captain Prince's "four hundred prisoners" of 1889: the 15th's muster rolls, made soon after the battle, show 84 captured without wounds; if every man of the 47th's seven companies (154) were counted the two regiments would give but 238, and at least 120 of the 47th escaped. He says Chamberlain "fell into the same error". His report of 8 August 1863 gives the 15th's whole loss as 17 killed, 54 wounded and 90 missing, "most of whom are either killed or wounded". He does not count the Texans or the brigade's other regiments; a private of the brigade, Norton (1913), answers that the 400 was a brigade total and "does not seem excessive", a reading that differs from Chamberlain's report and from Rice's over 500, and is kept as its own value. The muster rolls were not seen here.

**218.** No writer gives it. The plan took it from a web page that could not be read. The nearest figure is Oates's ceiling of 238, which is a ceiling, not a count.

## The weighing

- The reports of 1863 are the best class. Chamberlain was at the ledge and gives a round figure; Barnes gives an exact one but was elsewhere and relays. The two differ by 32 (within 9 per cent); a round 400 for a count of 368 is possible but nobody says so. Nothing separates them, so the default is their range, not a point.
- The regiment's survivors give about 300, the lowest figures by men who were there; they wrote 19 to 35 years later and name no source, so they rank below the reports of 1863.
- Oates's evidence is the only independent Confederate check. His rolls come to us only through a book of 1905, but his own report of 1863 fits them: the whole 15th lost 90 missing, most dead or wounded. It does not contradict a count for the brigade's whole front, which Chamberlain says included Texans and others.
- The 25 and the over 30 belong to the night advance; Rice's over 500 to the whole brigade. They are different counts and stay beside the default.
- The reported 20-minute difference between the armies' clocks bears on none of these counts.

## The decision

The default is 368 to 400 prisoners sent to the rear after the counter-charge, shown as a range with no single figure; the 25 of the night advance are shown beside it. Both ends are flagged single-source (each rests on one report, and nobody says how it was counted). The plan's range of 218 to 368 does not hold: 218 has no source and the range leaves out Chamberlain's 400. The first delegated default (368 to 425) holds in part: its 425 adds the night's 25 to the 400, so the shown range stops at 400.

## What would change it

The Confederate muster rolls, or the Union provost's receipt for the prisoners delivered to the rear on 2 and 3 July, would give a count that does not depend on Chamberlain's or Barnes's word. A report of the 47th Alabama or the Texas regiments with a missing figure would test the claim that the prisoners came mainly from the 15th and 47th. The source behind the plan's 218 would add that value.

## Searches that failed

No count of 218 was found in any local text. No other Confederate report gives a count of men captured: Campbell (47th Alabama) says about one third of his men were killed and wounded; Bane, Bryan and Scruggs give none. Not read: the Stone Sentinels page the plan names, Prince's address of 1889 (known through Norton and Oates), and the muster rolls Oates examined.
