# C-034: how many wagons were taken at Monterey Pass (night of July 4-5)

**The dispute.** The plan lists 40, 150, 300 and 400-500 wagons and recommends "150 (range shown)". The first delegated default showed 40 to 500. The figures count different things and come from different people.

**What each figure counts, and who wrote it.**

- *About 40.* Lee, to President Davis, 7 July 1863 (the letter's dateline is not printed; the next letter is dated 8 July and opens 'My letter of yesterday'): "In sending back our trains in advance, that of General Ewell was cut by the enemy's cavalry, and a number of wagons, said to be about 40, were captured." Stuart (cavalry commander, who passed the pass the next day; report after the campaign): a citizen told him Kilpatrick's men had claimed several thousand prisoners and 400 or 500 wagons, "but I was further informed that not more than 40 wagons accompanied them". Kilpatrick (report of 10 August 1863): Ewell's train "was entirely destroyed, save eight forges, thirty wagons, and a few ambulances". These three agree on a small number of wagons that went off with the Union column: 30 to 40.
- *150.* Huey (Colonel, 8th Pennsylvania Cavalry, a brigade at the rear of Kilpatrick's column; report of 6 August 1863): "At 3 a. m. July 5, the wagon train was reached at and near Monterey, and 150 wagons captured." A count of wagons captured, said without a stated method.
- *300.* Capehart (Major, 1st West Virginia Cavalry, which led the charge on the train in the dark; report of 17 August 1863): "the whole train was taken: 300 wagons, 15 ambulances, together with all the horses and mules attached", prisoners 1,300.
- *400-500.* Only Stuart's report of what a citizen said Kilpatrick had claimed. Kilpatrick's own dispatch of 5 July (received 1.45 p.m.) says the train from the mountains to Ridgeville was in his possession, the wagons of Ewell's corps destroyed, over 1,500 prisoners; his report says 1,360 prisoners; neither gives 400 or 500.
- *Later figures.* A magazine of 1864: "more than one hundred wagons". A guide of 1892: "over two hundred wagons" destroyed. A medical history of the 1910s: "37 wagons and 15 ambulances" said to be Kilpatrick's report; the figure is in none of Kilpatrick's own writings that are local. The plan's timeline note of "about 250" and "37 of Rodes's wagons" has no source in the volumes read.

**Weighing.** Huey and Capehart are two officers of Kilpatrick's column who wrote within weeks, were present, and give wagons captured or taken; they are the best class of writing and they disagree by a factor of two (and Huey's prisoners, 1,500 or 1,600 by the scan, are more than the whole division's 1,360). Nothing in the rank rules separates them: neither says how he counted, in the dark, over a road eight miles long. So the record is divided between 150 and 300. The 40 and the 30 count something else, the wagons carried away; the 400-500 is hearsay of a claim. The Union and Confederate clocks do not enter into a count.

**Decision.** Class irreducible, tier documented for the range: 150 to 300 wagons captured or destroyed on the night of 4-5 July, with only about 30 to 40 carried off, the claim of 400-500 and the later figures shown beside it. The picture draws 150 wagons (Huey's figure, the lower of the two eyewitness counts, and the plan's); that is a drawing choice and not a count, and 300 stays selectable. The plan's default (150, range shown) holds as the lower end; the first delegated default (40 to 500) does not hold, because it joins different counts and a hearsay claim.

**What would change it.** A Confederate return of the wagons lost (Ewell's quartermaster), a count by Kilpatrick or Custer of the train taken, or the missing page of a Union report that counts them.

**Searches that failed.** No Confederate count of wagons lost on the night was found (Lee's report of 1864 says "a number of wagons and ambulances"; Rodes says only that the train was partly destroyed). Kilpatrick's report has no count of the wagons destroyed. The plan's 250 and 37 of Rodes's wagons are in no source read.
