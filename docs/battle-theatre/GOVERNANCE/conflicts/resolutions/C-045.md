# C-045: Wind on Jul 2-3

**Result: gap. The wind speed is not recorded by any source of the time. Default: a light wind drawn at 2 to 6 mph, from the south to south-southwest, shown as a marked stand-in (plausible-procedural). 12 mph, 2 mph and "2 mph (light)" stay selectable.**

## The dispute

The plan's register lists two speeds for the wind of 2 and 3 July, 12 mph and 2 mph, and recommends 2 mph (light). The first delegated default (11 November) kept a light wind drawn at 2 to 6 mph, marked as a gap.

## Where the two figures come from

Both appear to come from one printed article (an inference from web pages), "Meteorology of the Battle", signed H. E. J., in the Gettysburg Star and Sentinel in 1885, in which Rev. Dr. Henry E. Jacobs reports the readings his father, Prof. Michael Jacobs of Pennsylvania College, made in 1863. Two web transcriptions of it read the first day's wind as "(2 miles per hour)" or 12; the project's weather record says the dispute is in the paragraph for 1 July, that 2 July says the wind was as on the day before, and that 3 July gives a direction ("very gentle", south-southwest) and no speed. I did not find the article: the Star and Sentinel of 1885 was not among the pages reachable, and the web pages are not sources of the time. How the speed was obtained (estimate or instrument) is not stated in anything I read. The 12 and the 2 therefore have no source of the time; a modern guess is not period evidence.

## What the people of the time say (words, not speeds)

- **Michael Jacobs, 1864** (resident, eyewitness; book published within a year): of the morning of Thursday 2 July, "The morning was pleasant, the air was calm, the sun shone mildly through a smoky atmosphere" (p. 82). His book prints no thermometer table and no wind speed (searched).
- **Arthur Fremantle, diary of 2 July** (British officer with Longstreet; kept day by day, printed 1863 and in the 1864 book): at the cannonade that began at 4.45 p. m., "A dense smoke arose for six miles; there was little wind to drive it away" (p. 259).
- **Kathleen Georg, National Park Service, 1981**: Jacobs "noted that day that the very gentle winds in the Gettysburg area were out of the south-southwest" (p. 69 of the PDF), about 3 July; she cites the article of 1885. This is a relay, not Jacobs himself, and gives no figure.
- **Frank Haskell, letter written soon after the battle**: just before the attack on 3 July, "the west wind kissed it" (the flag). A direction at one moment, possibly a figure of speech; it differs from the south-southwest. - **Samuel Toombs, 1888** (a later historian, no witness named): at the start of Longstreet's attack on 2 July, "The wind blowing directly from Ewell carried the sound of Longstreet's artillery and the Union batteries replying to it, to the southwest, so that he did not hear it at all." A wind from the north-northeast, against the south-southwest of Jacobs; direction only, one late source, so it stays a weak selectable value.
- **"No breeze during the Charge"** (3 July): carried in the plan's weather text and effects tiers as a summary of Jacobs; I found no source that states it (the 1885 article was not found), so it is listed as an unsourced value; Haskell's flag and the Park Service's "very gentle" winds point to a light breeze.

## Weighing

The best writing (a diary, a letter, a book of 1864) says light, calm or little, and not one of them gives a number. The two figures come from a source of 1885 that I could not read, in two readings that disagree, and neither has a non-lead source. Nothing separates 2 from 12 by the rubric: the words point to a lighter wind, but no source says what "calm" or "very gentle" meant in miles per hour, so I do not exclude 12. A wind at one place and hour is also not the wind of the whole field. The speed is a gap; the lightness is documented in words.

## Decision

Class gap, tier plausible-procedural. Default: a light wind drawn between 2 and 6 mph, a marked stand-in; it takes in the plan's 2 mph and stays below 12. The PDF default "2 mph (light)" holds as to "light" and not as a documented figure; the first delegated default holds. Nine other values are listed: 12 mph, 2 mph, "2 mph (light)", and six wordings (calm, little wind, very gentle from the south-southwest, west wind, wind from Ewell's side, no breeze during the Charge). The direction is not settled by the record: south-southwest (3 July only), west (3 July) and north-northeast (2 July) all appear, and nothing of the time gives one for 2 July. The Park Service note ranks above the stand-in as writing but is not the default: it relays an 1885 article and gives a word and a direction, not a speed. Open register entries: EG-FX-G09, EG-0019, EG-0257, DEC-C-045.

## Searches that failed

Official Records parts 1 to 3 (no statement about the wind of 1-4 July); Jacobs 1864 (no speed or table); the Smithsonian report for 1863 (fetched; it lists an observer at Gettysburg and prints no readings); Chronicling America and archive.org searches for the 1885 article (nothing relevant); the web transcriptions (not read here); the printed Star and Sentinel (not located).

## What would change it

A page of the 1885 article or Jacobs's original sheets that gives the wind in miles per hour, or a report, diary or letter of 2-3 July 1863 with a speed or an instrument reading. Either would settle 2 against 12.
